# Interval training by target distance: role, timing, and scheduling

## TL;DR
- **The role of VO2max interval work scales with how close race effort sits
  to VO2max.** 5K races run at ~95-98% VO2max and 10K at ~90-94%, so
  interval (I/R) work earns a real share of those plans; marathon races run
  at ~75-84% VO2max, so marathon plans lean on threshold/marathon-pace
  volume instead, with interval work in a smaller, maintenance role
  (Daniels' Running Formula, 3rd ed. 2014).
- **There is no evidence-based basis for gating interval work behind an
  absolute weekly-volume number like 42 km/wk.** The literature that
  actually studies *when* to safely progress intensity in novices gates on
  *weeks of consistent, injury-free base training* and *experience/training
  age*, not a specific mileage threshold — and the one RCT that directly
  tests intensity-progression risk (Run Clever) found no elevated injury
  risk from progressing intensity vs. volume, but only *after* every
  participant had already completed an identical 8-week base period
  (Ramskov et al. 2018, *JOSPT*; Malisoux et al. 2016, *BMC Musculoskelet
  Disord*).
- **Novice runners carry roughly 2x the baseline injury risk of experienced
  runners regardless of what quality work they do** (Videbæk et al. 2015;
  Kemler et al. 2018) — which argues for a longer, experience-tiered base
  requirement before intensity, not a volume number that happens to be
  reachable faster by fitter/returning runners.
- **Interval-specific session frequency should be capped low regardless of
  distance:** VO2max adaptations plateau at ~1-2 dedicated sessions/week,
  and a controlled overload trial found 3x/week produced no extra benefit
  over 1x/week while risking overtraining (Billat et al. 1999).

## Key Findings

### A — Role of interval work by target distance

1. **Daniels' Running Formula (3rd ed., 2014)** defines training paces as
   %VDOT bands — Easy 59-74%, Marathon 75-84%, Threshold 83-88%, Interval
   95-100%, Repetition >100% — and a standard weekly composition of
   ~70-80% Easy, ~10-15% Marathon+Threshold, ~10-15% Interval+Repetition.
   Race effort itself sits close to these same VO2max fractions by
   distance: 5K ~95-98% VO2max, 10K ~90-94%, marathon-pace running ~75-84%
   VO2max — i.e. the shorter the target race, the closer race effort sits
   to Interval pace, which is the physiological rationale for weighting
   interval work more heavily for 5K/10K goals and threshold/marathon-pace
   volume more heavily for half/marathon goals.
2. **Interval bout length determines the VO2max stimulus, independent of
   distance goal** (already cited in `onboarding-und-trainingsmethodik.md`
   Block A): Seiler & Sjursen (2004) found 3-5 min bouts reach ~92% VO2max
   vs. only ~82% for 1-min bouts; Seiler et al. (2013, 35 athletes) found
   4x8min (+11.4%) beat 4x4min (+5.5%) and 4x16min (+5.6%) — a ~8 min
   "sweet spot." Billat's classical VO2max session format (Billat 2001,
   *Sports Medicine* review) is 3-5 min repeats at vVO2max totalling
   20-30 min of work per session — this session *shape* doesn't change by
   target distance, only how often it appears in the week.
3. **Distance-specific training-intensity-distribution (TID) research.**
   Rosenblat et al. (2025, *Sports Medicine*, individual-data network
   meta-analysis, 13 studies/348 athletes — already cited in
   `onboarding-und-trainingsmethodik.md`) found no significant overall
   difference between polarized (POL), pyramidal (PYR), threshold, and
   high-volume models for VO2max/time-trial performance, but a subgroup
   signal (p<0.05) that athletes closer to competition benefit more from
   POL and recreational runners more from PYR. Esteve-Lanao, Casado et al.
   (2026, *randomized controlled study in recreational marathoners*, load
   matched between arms) directly compared POL (82/8/10% zone 1/2/3) vs.
   PYR (75/22/3%) for marathon performance. A 2025 machine-learning-based
   RCT-derived study of 120 recreational marathoners (Hanyang University)
   found POL produced a larger marathon-time improvement than PYR (11.3
   vs. 8.7 min, p<0.03) despite lower volume — but also found four
   distinct responder phenotypes (POL-responders 31.5%, PYR-responders
   31.9%, dual-responders 18.7%, non-responders 17.9%), i.e. a sizeable
   share of runners do better on the *opposite* model from the group
   average. **Net read: pyramidal (more threshold/marathon-pace volume,
   less pure VO2max work) is the more common and defensible default for
   half/marathon goals, but the evidence for a hard per-distance TID rule
   is genuinely mixed, not settled** (see Caveats).
4. **Billat's frequency findings by goal.** Billat's overload study (1999,
   8 subjects): 4 weeks of 1x/week vVO2max training significantly improved
   velocity at VO2max (20.5→21.1 km/h, p=0.02) via improved running
   economy; a subsequent 4 weeks of 3x/week vVO2max sessions changed
   neither performance nor its underlying factors — i.e. more frequent
   dedicated VO2max work did not outperform 1x/week, and Billat's own
   recommendation caps dedicated VO2max work at ~1 session/week. Midgley
   and colleagues (cited secondary synthesis) similarly found VO2max
   responds to 1-2 weekly sessions in this intensity range with
   diminishing returns beyond that — supporting a low, fixed
   sessions/week cap for interval work *regardless of target distance*,
   with distance instead changing the *share of the week* interval pace
   occupies, not how many hard interval sessions/week are scheduled.

### B — When intensity should be introduced: is there a volume gate?

1. **No RCT ties intensity-introduction readiness to an absolute weekly
   distance.** The literature that does study "how fast can/should a
   novice add intensity" gates on *weeks of consistent training* and
   *training age*, not a km/week number:
   - **Run Clever / Ramskov et al. (2018, *J Orthop Sports Phys Ther*
     48(10):740-748)**, built on Malisoux et al.'s trial design (2016,
     *BMC Musculoskelet Disord*): 839 recreational runners completed an
     identical **8-week preconditioning (base) period**, after which 447
     were randomized into a schedule progressing running *intensity*
     (Sch-I) vs. one progressing running *volume* (Sch-V), with weekly
     training load otherwise matched. Result (companion paper, *BMJ Open
     Sport & Exerc Med* 2018): 80 total injuries (Sch-I n=36, Sch-V n=44);
     risk differences between arms for both intensity-related and
     volume-related injuries had confidence intervals crossing zero at
     every follow-up — **no significant difference in injury risk between
     progressing intensity vs. progressing volume.** This is the closest
     available analogue to a "RUNSAFE-style rule for intensity
     introduction," and its own trial *design* embeds an 8-week base
     period as the prerequisite before either arm progresses — not a
     volume threshold.
   - **Kluitenberg et al. (2016, *J Sci Med Sport* 19(8):642-6, the
     NLstart2run training-factors study, 1,696 novices in a 6-week
     Start-to-Run program):** higher self-reported intensity (RPE) in the
     preceding week was associated with higher injury risk; frequency was
     not significantly associated with injury; counterintuitively, *lower*
     weekly volume was associated with higher injury risk. The authors
     explicitly caution this is likely confounded and "should not be used
     plainly as a guideline for novices" — cited here for completeness,
     not as a positive rule to build on.
   - **Videbæk et al. (2015, *Sports Medicine* 45(7):1017-1026),
     meta-analysis of 13 studies:** injury incidence 17.8/1000h (95% CI
     16.7-19.1) in novice runners vs. 7.7/1000h (95% CI 6.9-8.7) in
     recreational runners — **novices carry roughly 2.3x the baseline
     injury risk**, independent of any specific training variable.
   - **Kemler et al. (2018, *Physician and Sportsmedicine* 46(4):485-491,
     n=4,621, 1,405 of them novice):** 4-year average injury incidence
     8.78/1000h (novice) vs. 4.24/1000h (experienced) — **novices ~2x the
     risk of experienced runners**, consistent with Videbæk.
2. **Coaching-literature consensus on a base-building runway** (secondary/
   practitioner sources, not RCT-tested, included here because they
   converge with the RCT design above): commonly 6-9 weeks of consistent,
   mostly-easy running before structured speed work for a genuine
   beginner, sometimes stated as up to 12 weeks; readiness markers are
   *stable weekly mileage for 3+ weeks* and *no active injury flags*,
   rather than a specific distance reached. The Lydiard method's classic
   sequencing (8-10 weeks aerobic base → hill strengthening → interval/
   anaerobic work) is built on the same logic: intensity should follow
   base-established aerobic and musculoskeletal capacity, tracked in time,
   not in kilometers.

### C — Beginner vs. returning/trained runner

- The elevated novice injury risk (Block B, Videbæk/Kemler) is a *training-
  age* effect, not a volume effect — a returning trained runner at the same
  weekly volume as a true beginner does not carry the same risk profile,
  because (per `progression-und-verletzungspraevention.md` §A2) their
  cardiovascular/muscular systems retain "muscle memory" and rebuild
  faster, even though their passive structures (bone/tendon) still need
  the same real-time remodeling window.
- No literature found ties *intensity* readiness specifically to
  experience tier the way `progression-und-verletzungspraevention.md`
  ties *volume* build rate to it — but the injury-incidence gap (Block B)
  is the direct evidence base for applying a **longer base-period
  requirement for beginners than for returners/continuous runners**,
  matching what `onboarding-und-trainingsmethodik.md` already prescribes
  qualitatively ("true beginner" mode = even more conservative) without
  currently being implemented anywhere in the phase-gate logic.

### D — Session structure and weekly scheduling

- **Frequency cap:** 1-2 dedicated hard sessions/week, with Billat's
  overload study (Block A4) specifically supporting that a *third*
  dedicated VO2max session/week adds no measured benefit and only
  overtraining risk. This is consistent with the engine's existing
  `alloc.max_two_quality` cap, but suggests that of the two allowed
  quality slots, only one should typically be a true VO2max-interval
  session — the other is better spent on threshold/tempo work, which
  carries a lower relative intensity and correspondingly lower per-session
  risk.
- **Session volume:** Billat's classical format totals 20-30 min of work
  at vVO2max per session (Block A2) — directionally consistent with the
  engine's existing `session.interval_structure` heuristic (interval
  session sized as ~15% of weekly volume, clamped 3-10 km), though that
  clamp is an engine heuristic, not independently derived from Billat.
- **Placement:** none of the sources reviewed here add anything beyond
  what's already encoded in `alloc.max_two_quality` and
  `days.no_back_to_back_hard` (≥2 days between quality sessions and the
  long run) — hard/easy spacing is a shared principle across all the
  reviewed intensity literature, not something that varies by distance.
- **Progression across a block:** for shorter goals (5K/10K), interval
  work is typically maintained at something close to its introduction
  frequency through most of the build, tapering in volume (not
  eliminated) in race week alongside all other intensity, per
  `volume.taper` (Bosquet et al. 2007, already in the engine). For
  half/marathon goals, interval work should play a smaller, more
  intermittent "maintenance of the aerobic ceiling" role once threshold/
  marathon-pace volume becomes the dominant quality component — this
  specific cadence claim (often stated informally as "roughly every 1-2
  weeks" for marathon-specific interval maintenance) is a coaching-derived
  synthesis from the sources above, not a directly cited trial finding,
  and should be treated as a heuristic, not evidence.

### E — Real-world 5K plans as a practical cross-check

Tempora's flagship goal is a 5K (see `CLAUDE.md`), so two published 5K plans
were reviewed as a design-practice cross-check against the RCT/cohort
evidence in Block B — not peer-reviewed, but useful for how mainstream
coaching actually operationalizes "when does speed work start":

1. **Nike Run Club, 8-week 5K Training Program** (nike.com PDF). No volume
   gate anywhere in the plan. Structured "Speed Run" work (their umbrella
   term for intervals/fartlek/hills/tempo) starts in **week 1** — the very
   first speed session is `8x1:00 @ 5K pace, 1:00 recovery`, scheduled right
   after nothing more than a 5-minute recovery run. Sessions are prescribed
   by **duration, not distance** (minutes, not km) throughout, and only the
   duration/complexity of the speed work progresses week to week (up to
   `4x1200m @ 5K pace, 3min recovery`-equivalent efforts by race week), while
   the long run separately grows from ~10 min to ~33 min. The plan explicitly
   states it's built to "adapt to your experience level" and can be started
   with as little as 4 weeks to race day; its only stated prerequisite is
   that you "can comfortably run and complete the programmed workouts" — a
   subjective readiness/comfort criterion, not a mileage number.
2. **runningfront.com, 16-week 5K training plan (DE)**. A periodized 3-phase
   structure — base (~6 wk) / build (~6 wk) / race-specific (~4 wk). Genuine
   VO2max-pace intervals are held back until Phase 2, but the gate is
   **elapsed weeks of base training completed**, not a volume number; Phase 1
   already includes light intensity (hill sprints, 2x/week). Once unlocked,
   quality work caps at two sessions/week, matching the engine's existing
   `alloc.max_two_quality`. Long-run progression (75 → 90 → 105 min on a
   repeating 6-week cycle) again runs on its own track, independent of when
   speed work starts.

Neither plan reaches for an absolute km/week threshold anywhere — one
introduces light speed work immediately (duration-gated, subjective-comfort
gated), the other gates on elapsed base-phase weeks. Both land on the same
structural pattern as the RCT/cohort evidence in Block B: **time-in-program
and/or subjective readiness, not a distance number, is what mainstream
practice actually uses to decide when speed work starts** — an independent
line of support for Recommendation (a), from an entirely different evidence
class (commercial/practitioner plan design) than the RCT/cohort literature.

For a 5K goal specifically, this also argues for keeping the base-period gate
(Recommendation a) toward the **shorter/lighter end** of the beginner range
and introducing speed work as short, low-volume, duration-capped efforts
first (Nike's ~1 min work intervals) before progressing toward longer
VO2max-pace repeats — rather than delaying intensity until a large volume or
many-week milestone, since 5K race effort sits closest to Interval pace of
any target distance (Block A1) and both reviewed real-world plans treat
early, light speed exposure as compatible with a beginner-adaptable plan.

## Recommendations

### (a) Replace the absolute-km phase gates with a base-period + experience gate

Neither `tempoKm: 28` nor `intervalKm: 42` (current `PHASE_VOLUME_GATES`)
has direct support in the literature as a safety threshold. What the
evidence actually converges on:

1. **Gate on weeks of consistent base training completed, tiered by
   experience**, not on an absolute km/week number:
   - beginner: ~8-10 weeks of consistent, mostly-easy running before
     tempo/threshold work; ~10-12 weeks before VO2max interval work
     (upper end of the coaching-consensus range in Block B2, justified by
     beginners' ~2x baseline injury risk in Block B1).
   - returner/continuous: ~4-6 weeks before tempo/threshold; ~6-8 weeks
     before interval work (lower end of the same range, justified by
     faster cardiovascular/muscular rebuild in returners per
     `progression-und-verletzungspraevention.md` §A2).
   - "Consistent" should require both elapsed weeks *and* no recent
     deload triggered by a pain-traffic-light or injury flag, mirroring
     Run Clever's design precedent of an uninterrupted base period before
     progressing intensity, and the coaching-literature readiness markers
     (stable mileage, injury-free) in Block B2.
2. **If a volume-based check is kept at all, make it relative, not
   absolute:** e.g. a fraction of the runner's own target/goal volume
   (already how `longrun.spike_ceiling` and the allocation rules scale to
   the individual) rather than a fixed constant that happens to sit above
   every distance's target volume in `WEEKLY_VOLUME_TARGET_BY_DISTANCE_METERS`
   for 5K but could be unreachable-in-time for lower-volume goals — this
   would keep a volume safety net without reproducing the current flaw of
   one persona's table value being applied universally.
3. **Keep tempo unlocking before interval** (already correct in relative
   ordering) since threshold intensity is uniformly treated as
   lower-risk/more foundational across every source reviewed here.

### (b) Vary interval:tempo:easy composition by target distance once unlocked

- **5K/10K goals:** once unlocked, allow interval work toward the upper
  end of Daniels' 10-15% Interval+Repetition band (or lean more
  polarized), reflecting these races' effort sitting at 90-98% VO2max.
- **Half/marathon goals:** favor threshold/marathon-pace volume (pyramidal
  lean) over interval share, consistent with marathon race effort sitting
  at 75-84% VO2max and with the majority of the TID evidence favoring
  pyramidal for marathon-distance goals (Block A3) — while flagging to
  the runner, honestly, that the TID-by-distance evidence is directional,
  not settled (see Caveats).
- **Frequency, not just share:** cap dedicated VO2max-interval sessions at
  1/week regardless of distance (Block A4); a second weekly quality slot,
  when used, should default to threshold/tempo rather than a second
  interval session.

### F — A deterministic interval-prescription rule: Daniels' system, and a derived formula

**Short answer: yes, an existing deterministic rule exists — Daniels' Running
Formula (3rd ed., 2014) — and it is more specific than anything currently in
the engine.** It maps zone (already computed from VDOT via
`ZONE_PERCENT_BANDS`) and the runner's own weekly volume to an exact session
cap, rep-duration bound, and work:rest ratio, for each quality-pace type:

| Pace | %VDOT (already in `ZONE_PERCENT_BANDS`) | Per-session volume cap | Individual rep-duration bound | Work:rest ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Threshold (T) | 83-88% | ~10% of weekly mileage | continuous or long "cruise" intervals (no hard cap) | short recoveries (~1 min) if broken into cruise intervals |
| Interval (I) | 95-100% | lesser of 10K and 8% of weekly mileage | 3-5 min (no benefit past 5 min — avoids anaerobic/lactate spillover) | recovery ≈ or slightly less than work time (~1:1) |
| Repetition (R) | 105-120% | lesser of 8km/5mi and 5% of weekly mileage | ≤ 2 min | recovery 2-3x work time, or an equal-distance jog |

These figures are corroborated consistently across multiple independent
secondary sources summarizing Daniels' book (Coach Ray, teesche.com's book
review, Fellrnr's wiki, and others) — they converge tightly enough (8%/10K
for I, 5%/8km for R, 3-5min/≤2min bounds, 1:1 vs 1:2-3 ratios all repeated
verbatim across unrelated sources) to be treated as an accurate restatement
of the book's actual rules, but none of them is the primary text itself, so
flagged as **secondary-source-corroborated, not independently verified
against the primary book** (see Caveats).

**This gives the engine something Recommendation (a) doesn't need but
`session.interval_structure` does: a genuinely deterministic function**, not
just a threshold. Because the caps are *percentages of the runner's own
current weekly volume* rather than fixed constants, the prescription scales
automatically as volume builds week to week — no separate progression table
is needed; progression falls out of the existing volume curve for free.

**Algorithmic prior art.** A Nike, Inc. patent family ("Adaptive athletic
activity prescription systems," priority 2016; e.g. US 11,177,037 B2 — the
same Nike behind the NRC 5K plan reviewed in Block E) describes a
TRIMP/critical-velocity/anaerobic-work-capacity (Banister-style
fitness-fatigue impulse-response model, with athletic constants K1/K2/T1/T2)
system that generates candidate interval workouts (rep count, distance,
pace, rest) filtered against numeric constraints — search-result summaries
of the claims describe a rest-duration bound of 30 seconds to 5 minutes and
an anaerobic-work-capacity depletion filter of 10-50% of the athlete's
store, though the primary claims text couldn't be directly verified (PDF
OCR was unreadable; see Caveats). This is useful as confirmation that a
deterministic, formula-driven approach to interval prescription is
established engineering practice, not merely academic — but it should be
cited as **prior art on the approach**, not as a scientific source for any
specific number.

**Synthesizing a concrete formula for `session.interval_structure`,**
combining Daniels' caps/bounds above with what Blocks A2/A4/D already
established (Seiler & Sjursen's bout-length-vs-%VO2max curve, Seiler et
al.'s ~8min sweet spot, Billat's 1/week frequency cap, the target-distance
composition lean from Recommendation (b)):

```
given: zonePaceSecPerKm (from ZONE_PERCENT_BANDS, already computed from VDOT),
       weeklyVolumeKm, sessionType ("interval" | "repetition" | "threshold")

1. sessionCapKm =
     interval:   min(10, 0.08 * weeklyVolumeKm)
     repetition: min(8,  0.05 * weeklyVolumeKm)
     threshold:  0.10 * weeklyVolumeKm

2. repDurationTargetMin =
     interval:   3-5 (Daniels' hard cap; Seiler's data supports allowing
                 up to ~8 for more trained runners/longer target distances —
                 an engine design choice that knowingly extends past
                 Daniels' cap, not a citation — see Caveats)
     repetition: <=2

3. repDistanceKm = round to a "clean" distance (200/400/800/1000/1200/1600m)
   such that repDistanceKm * zonePaceSecPerKm/3600 lands inside the
   repDurationTargetMin band

4. repCount = floor(sessionCapKm / repDistanceKm), clamped to a sane
   minimum (e.g. >=3) so low-volume weeks don't produce a degenerate session

5. restSec =
     interval:   ~0.8-1.0 * repDurationSec        (Daniels: ~= work time)
     repetition: ~2-3 * repDurationSec, or jog the rep distance

6. sessionsPerWeek: interval capped at 1/week regardless of target distance
   (Block A4); the second `alloc.max_two_quality` slot, when used, defaults
   to threshold rather than a second interval session, except optionally in
   a 5K/10K race-specific phase — flagged as a thinner-evidence extension,
   not itself cited (see Caveats)
```

Everything in steps 1, 2 (the 3-5/≤2 bounds), and 5 is Daniels' cited rule;
steps 3-4 (translating a duration/rest rule into a concrete rep-distance and
rep-count) and the >=8min extension in step 2 are engine-heuristic synthesis
built to be *consistent with* the cited rules, not themselves independently
tested — same honesty split the existing `RULE_SOURCES` convention already
uses elsewhere in this codebase (e.g. `"engine heuristic — no direct
citation"` vs. a real citation).

## Caveats
- **No RCT directly tests "should novices delay quality work until X
  km/week."** The 6-12-week base-building consensus in Block B2 is
  practitioner/coaching consensus, not an RCT finding — it's included
  because it converges with the *design* of the one relevant RCT (Run
  Clever's 8-week preconditioning period) and with the novice-vs-experienced
  injury-incidence gap (Videbæk, Kemler), not because it's independently
  proven.
- **Run Clever's null result doesn't validate skipping a base phase.** Its
  participants had already completed 8 weeks of preconditioning before
  either arm progressed intensity or volume — the trial shows that,
  *given* a base, progressing intensity isn't uniquely riskier than
  progressing volume. It does not test what happens with no base at all.
- **The TID-by-distance picture is genuinely mixed, not settled.**
  Rosenblat et al.'s primary finding was *no* significant overall
  difference between POL/PYR/threshold/high-volume models — only a
  subgroup signal favored PYR for recreational runners. A 2025
  machine-learning marathon study found POL outperformed PYR on average,
  directly cutting against the "PYR for marathon" convention used in
  Recommendation (b), and identified large minority responder groups
  (~32% each) who do better on the model *opposite* their assigned one.
  Treat distance-based TID guidance as a reasonable default, not a rule to
  enforce rigidly or cite to a user as settled science.
- **Kluitenberg et al.'s counterintuitive volume finding** (lower volume →
  higher injury risk in novices) is explicitly flagged by its own authors
  as likely confounded; it's documented here for completeness, not as
  supporting evidence for any rule in this doc.
- **Billat's frequency/overload finding (Block A4) comes from 8 subjects**
  — mechanistically informative and consistent with later diminishing-
  returns findings (Midgley), but not statistically powered like the
  injury-incidence cohorts cited in Block B.
- **The "marathon interval work roughly every 1-2 weeks" cadence claim in
  Block D** is a coaching-derived synthesis across sources, not a number
  from any single cited study — flagged explicitly so it isn't later
  treated as a citable figure the way `intervalKm: 42` was.
- **Block E's two real-world 5K plans (Nike, runningfront.com) are
  commercial/enthusiast sources, not peer-reviewed** — included as a
  design-practice cross-check on Recommendation (a), not as evidence with
  the same weight as the RCT/cohort citations in Block B.
- **Block F's Daniels I/R/T percentage-cap and rep-duration figures are
  corroborated across several independent secondary sources but were not
  read directly from the primary book text** — treat as a high-confidence
  restatement, not a verbatim-verified quote, and re-verify against the
  book directly before treating any single figure (e.g. the exact 8%
  vs. 10% boundary) as load-bearing for a safety-relevant clamp.
- **Daniels' 5-minute I-pace rep cap and Seiler et al.'s ~8-minute
  VO2max-stimulus sweet spot (Block A2) are in tension**, not agreement —
  Daniels caps reps at 5 min specifically to avoid anaerobic/lactate
  contribution, while Seiler's data shows a *better* VO2max stimulus at
  longer bouts. Block F's synthesized formula extends past Daniels' cap for
  more trained runners as a design choice; this is an unresolved
  disagreement between two frameworks, not a settled number, and should be
  surfaced as such if the exact cutoff ever needs defending.
- **The Nike patent's numeric bounds (30s-5min rest, 10-50% anaerobic-work-
  capacity depletion) come from a search-engine's summary of the claims
  text, not a directly verified read of the primary document** — the PDF's
  OCR was unreadable via WebFetch. Cited only as evidence that a
  deterministic, formula-driven approach to interval prescription is
  established practice, not as a source for any specific number.

## Implications for `phase.tempo_gate` / `phase.interval_gate`

Summarizing Recommendation (a) for direct engine translation:

1. Replace the single `PHASE_VOLUME_GATES = { tempoKm, intervalKm }`
   absolute-km constant with a **weeks-of-consistent-base** gate, tiered
   by `experience` (the type already exists on the plan-engine input:
   `beginner | returner | continuous`):
   - `tempoGateWeeks`: beginner ~8-10, returner/continuous ~4-6
   - `intervalGateWeeks`: beginner ~10-12, returner/continuous ~6-8
   - "consistent" = no pain-traffic-light/injury-triggered deload in the
     lookback window (exact operationalization is an engine design
     choice, not something the cited literature specifies).
2. Optionally keep a relative-volume floor (e.g. a fraction of the
   runner's own target weekly volume) as a secondary safety check, not the
   primary gate — this avoids reintroducing an absolute constant that
   happens to fit only the original single-persona case.
3. Once unlocked, vary the interval share within Daniels' 10-15%
   Interval+Repetition band by target distance (toward the upper end for
   5K/10K, lower end plus more threshold/marathon-pace volume for
   half/marathon), and keep dedicated interval-session frequency at 1/week
   regardless of distance, independent of the composition change.

**Implemented, then corrected.** The first implementation used the
tempoGateWeeks/intervalGateWeeks split above (beginner 9/11, returner/
continuous 5/7). On review this reproduced the same mistake this whole
investigation started from: an uncited number (here, the 2-week gap between
tempo and interval) applied as if it were evidence-based, and it
contradicted this doc's own Section E finding — real-world beginner-oriented
5K plans (Nike) introduce multiple quality-work types together from week 1,
kept safe by small per-session doses rather than a staggered unlock
calendar. `constants.ts`'s `QUALITY_BASE_GATE_WEEKS` now uses a **single**
gate for both tempo and interval, set directly to Run Clever's actual
8-week (beginner) / 4-week (returner/continuous) preconditioning-period
design rather than a coaching-consensus range with an invented gap on top.
One consequence, deliberately accepted: since `phase.interval_gate` and
`phase.tempo_gate` now unlock the same week, `curve.ts`'s phase resolution
(`intervalUnlocked ? "interval" : tempoUnlocked ? ...`) means the `"tempo"`
`Phase` value is currently unreachable in practice — documented by a test in
`volume-curve.test.ts` rather than left as a silent surprise. The relative-
volume secondary floor (point 2 above) was not implemented, for the same
reason: no cited fraction exists to use, and inventing one would repeat the
pattern again.

## Implications for `session.interval_structure`

Summarizing Block F for direct engine translation. This replaces the flat
`QUALITY_SESSION = { shareOfWeeklyVolume: 0.15, minKm: 3, maxKm: 10 }`
heuristic (which doesn't distinguish interval/repetition/threshold or
produce a rep/rest structure at all) with the deterministic function
derived above:

1. **Per-pace-type session volume cap**, replacing the single
   `shareOfWeeklyVolume: 0.15` constant:
   - interval: `min(10km, 0.08 * weeklyVolumeKm)`
   - repetition: `min(8km, 0.05 * weeklyVolumeKm)`
   - threshold: `0.10 * weeklyVolumeKm`
   - This is a direct citation (Daniels), not a heuristic, and it already
     scales with the volume curve — no separate progression logic needed.
2. **Rep-duration bounds feed a rep-distance calculation** from the
   runner's own zone pace (already computed via `ZONE_PERCENT_BANDS`):
   interval 3-5 min (Daniels' cited cap; optionally extended toward
   Seiler's ~8min for trained runners/longer targets as a flagged design
   choice, not a citation), repetition ≤2 min.
3. **Rep count** falls out of `sessionCapKm / repDistanceKm`, clamped to a
   sane minimum so low-volume weeks don't produce a degenerate 1-2-rep
   session.
4. **Rest duration**: interval ≈ 0.8-1.0x work time; repetition ≈ 2-3x work
   time (or equal-distance jog) — both direct Daniels citations.
5. **Frequency**: keep `alloc.max_two_quality`'s existing 2/week cap, but
   add a same-family rule that only one of those two slots defaults to a
   true interval session (Billat, Block A4) — the other defaults to
   threshold, which is both lower-risk and, per Block A1, still the
   dominant quality component for every target distance except short-race
   goals late in a build.

None of this has been implemented — `constants.ts`, `session.ts` (or
wherever `session.interval_structure` lives), and `catalog.ts` are
unmodified. Implementing it means introducing a `QUALITY_SESSION_BY_TYPE`
(or similarly named) structure keyed by pace type instead of the current
single flat constant, and a new pure function analogous to the existing
`session.duration_from_distance` rule that turns
`(zonePaceSecPerKm, weeklyVolumeKm, sessionType)` into
`(repCount, repDistanceKm, restSec)`.
